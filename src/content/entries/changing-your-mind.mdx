There is a particular pleasure in discovering that you were wrong about something small. The café you walked past for months turns out to be lovely. The book you dismissed becomes a favorite. A conversation loosens an opinion you thought was settled.

Changing your mind can feel like losing ground. But sometimes it is how you find better ground.

## A belief is a starting point

Suppose you think a new route to work will be quicker. You try it on Monday and arrive ten minutes early. That is evidence, but it is not the whole story. Perhaps traffic was unusually light.

You try it again. And again. Gradually, your first guess becomes something more useful: a belief shaped by experience.

This is the intuition behind updating a belief. We begin somewhere, notice what happens, and adjust. We do not need to treat every new observation as a complete reset.

## Leave room for surprise

The useful question is less “Am I right?” and more “What would make me reconsider?” That question gives a belief some breathing room.

> Confidence can be a useful tool. Certainty is a much heavier thing to carry.

Try keeping a small list of expectations for a week. Write down what you think will happen, why you think it, and what actually happens. Keep the stakes low: a time estimate, a recipe, a meeting.

## A practice, not a verdict

The point is not to score every prediction. It is to notice the distance between the world in your head and the world outside it.

There is always another observation to make. That is good news.
